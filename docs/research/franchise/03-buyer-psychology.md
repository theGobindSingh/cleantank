# CT-409 · Thread 3: Buyer psychology for the `/franchise` page

Researcher 3 of 5. Scope: how a person decides to buy a small franchise, and how the page should be built around that decision, honestly.

How to read this:
- **Found** = what a study or source reports.
- **Inference** = my reading of what it means for us.
- **Unsourced** = a popular claim I could not trace to a primary source. Do not repeat it on the site or in decks.
- Classic studies (Kahneman & Tversky, Mayer et al., Spence, Petty & Cacioppo and so on) are cited from their standard bibliographic records. For several franchise papers I could see only the abstract or secondary citations; this is marked.

---

## 0. The short version

1. A franchise buyer is buying **reduced uncertainty**, not a business idea. Research on prospective franchisees puts return, training and "being my own boss" on top (Bennett, Frazer & Weaven 2010). Our page has to prove the system is real and teachable before it talks about money.
2. Trust is the opening move, and it has to be earned with **costly, checkable signals** (Spence 1973): 18 years of work, named institutional clients, ISO, GSTIN and Udyam, a real office, a named founder, written terms. Cheap signals (badges, adjectives, ROI percentages) do little, and in this category they read as fraud markers.
3. The buyer does not decide alone. In North India the spouse, father or elders are part of the decision. The page should hand the reader something to **show the family**.
4. Money should be shown as a **range with its parts, its conditions and its risks**, beside the disclaimer. Our current figures have an internal problem (§7.3) that a careful reader will spot. Fix it before launch.
5. Admitting limits raises credibility (two-sided messages, Eisend 2006). "Who this is not for" and "what can go wrong" sections are persuasion here, not modesty.
6. The conversion is not "buy". It is a **small, safe next step**: a WhatsApp chat, a call with the founder, an office visit. Respond fast (Oldroyd et al. 2011).
7. Reject every urgency, scarcity, income-guarantee and fake-proof tactic. They are illegal or risky under the CCPA guidelines, and they copy what the fraudsters do.

## 1. How people decide to buy a franchise

### 1.1 Why franchise, rather than a job or an independent business

**Found**
- **Kaufmann & Stanworth (1995)**, "The decision to purchase a franchise: a study of prospective franchisees", *Journal of Small Business Management* 33(4), 22–33. One of the few studies of prospective (not existing) franchisees. Later reviews cite it for framing the franchise choice as part of the wider self-employment decision. I could not retrieve the full findings. [EconBiz record](https://econbiz.de/Record/the-decision-to-purchase-a-franchise-a-study-of-prospective-franchisees-kaufmann-patrick/10005993721)
- **Kaufmann (1999)**, "Franchising and the choice of self-employment", *Journal of Business Venturing* 14(4), 345–362. Sample: people who had shown interest in self-employment and franchising three years earlier. The importance people gave to the **financial and business benefits of franchising** (proven format, brand, support) was positively linked to actually buying one. **Franchisees were less likely than independents to work in a sector where they had prior experience**: the franchise is how people enter an unfamiliar trade. [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jbvent/v14y1999i4p345-362.html)
- **Peterson & Dant (1990)**, "Perceived advantages of the franchise option from the franchisee perspective", *Journal of Small Business Management* 28(3). Grouped Izraeli's 17 motives into seven. Citing papers report: the established name and lower start-up costs were the main draws over going independent; **greater independence** was the main draw over employment; ease of finance and less management involvement were *not* seen as advantages. (Secondary; full text not retrieved.)
- **Bennett, Frazer & Weaven (2010)**, "What prospective franchisees are seeking", *Journal of Marketing Channels* 17(1), 69–87, doi:10.1080/10466690903436313. Australian trade-show attendees ranked **(1) a strong return on investment, (2) training from the franchisor, (3) being their own boss** as most important. They showed entrepreneurial traits but **still wanted ongoing support**. Trade shows were a key information source. [USC record](https://research.usc.edu.au/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/What-prospective-franchisees-are-seeking/99451478202621)
- **Guilloux, Gauzente, Kalika & Dubost (2004)**, "How France's potential franchisees reach their decisions: a comparison with franchisers' perceptions", *JSBM* 42(2), 218–224. Compares what prospects weigh with what franchisors think they weigh; the paper's premise is that the two differ. Findings not retrieved. [HAL](https://hal.archives-ouvertes.fr/hal-03219158)
- **Weaven, Frazer & Giddings (2010)**, "New perspectives on the causes of franchising conflict in Australia", *Asia Pacific Journal of Marketing and Logistics* 22(2), 135–155. Interviews with 24 experts. **Poor due diligence leads to unrealistic expectations, which lead to later conflict.** [Emerald](https://www.emerald.com/insight/content/doi/10.1108/13555851011026917/full/html)

**Indian evidence (thin)**
- **Kushwaha & Sakshi (2025)**, *International Journal of Business and Systems Research* 19(2), 203–220. Indian entrepreneurs choosing franchise over independent business: personal, social and economic factors matter; intrinsic motivation is significant. Abstract only. [RePEc](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:ids:ijbsre:v:19:y:2025:i:2:p:203-220)
- **Franchise India survey (Nov 2010)**, 1,000 prospective investors at north Indian expos (industry survey, not peer reviewed). **86% had no franchise experience; lack of experience was the biggest hurdle**; they expected the franchisor to handle marketing, training and advertising; fear of competition; dissatisfaction with corporate jobs was a driver; interest growing in B and C towns; 66% self-funded; investors over 40 benchmarked returns against the bank rate or "about 2% a month". Info sources then: newspapers 58%, SMS 14%, friends 7%, internet 2% (2010; the internet share is surely far higher now). [Franchise India](https://www.franchiseindia.com/magazine/2010/November/who-is-investing-why-where-how-much.666)

**Inference for us**
- Our buyer is buying a **way into a trade they don't know**. The page must show the system can be learned by an outsider: training content, days, who trains, what the first jobs look like.
- "Be your own boss" is a real motive, but research shows they **still want support**. Pitch "your business, our system" rather than pure independence.
- The 2010 north India detail that older investors compare returns with **bank interest or monthly income** matters: this buyer thinks in monthly rupees, not ROI percentages.
- Unrealistic expectations cause later conflict (Weaven et al. 2010). Honest framing protects the network too, not just the brand.

### 1.2 Stages and duration

**Found / unsourced**
- The general considered-purchase sequence (need, search, evaluation, decision, post-purchase) is textbook consumer behaviour (e.g. Engel, Kollat & Blackwell's model). I found no peer-reviewed study that times the franchise decision in India.
- **Unsourced:** industry blogs say a franchise decision "takes 3–6 months" or "people talk to 5+ franchisors". I could not trace these to a study. Don't quote them.

**Inference: a working journey for our buyer**
1. **Trigger**: job frustration, retirement from service, a return from abroad, idle savings, a shop owner wanting a second line.
2. **Browse**: many tabs, many sectors (the 2010 survey says prospects weigh three or four sectors). Scanning for "is this real, what does it cost".
3. **Shortlist**: reads one or two pages in depth; checks the company outside the site (Google Maps, GST lookup, YouTube, asks someone).
4. **Family round**: shows the spouse, father, a CA or a relative "in business". This is where many decisions die.
5. **Contact**: WhatsApp or a call, usually to test "is a real person there".
6. **Verify**: meeting, office visit, documents, perhaps a live job.
7. **Commit**: agreement, payment, training.

The page has to serve stages 2 to 5 at once. Most visitors will not convert on the first visit. That is normal, not a failure.

### 1.3 Who else decides

**Found**
- An ethnographic study of 32 members of six middle-class Delhi-NCR families (*Entrepreneurship Research Journal* 13(3), 725–771, 2023) found two family influences on entrepreneurial decisions: **generational congruence and the need to keep harmony**, plus pressure to conform to a social image. Small, Delhi-only sample. [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bpj/erjour/v13y2023i3p725-771n8.html)
- A 2024 Gujarat survey of 300 young people found family history and parents' outlook shaped business-owner intentions (summary only).
- Household investment in India leans on informal advice from family and friends; secondary reports of the SEBI–Nielsen 2015 survey give figures of 32% and 67% that conflict, so I won't quote a number. [SEBI report PDF](https://www.sebi.gov.in/sebi_data/attachdocs/1491452612271.pdf)

**Inference**
- Expect a **second reader**: the father, spouse or elder brother. They read for risk and status ("what will people say"), not opportunity. The page needs a section that answers the family, and a shareable summary (a one-page PDF or a WhatsApp-friendly card) in Hindi and Punjabi.
- A founder of the same generation as the father (our CEO) is an asset with the elder reader. Put his face and his invitation to meet near the end, where the family round happens.

## 2. The Indian small investor: risk, trust and fraud

### 2.1 Fraud is the background noise

**Found** (police and news reports; pattern is consistent):
- Udupi, Nov 2025: two arrested for a fake **Campa Cola** franchise site. [Deccan Herald](https://www.deccanherald.com/amp/story/india%2Fkarnataka%2Fkarnataka-cen-police-bust-inter-state-cyber-fraud-racket-two-arrested-3815215)
- Surat, Jul 2025: ₹31 lakh lost to a fake **Tata Zudio** franchise; asked for registration, NOC and agreement fees in instalments. [DeshGujarat](https://deshgujarat.com/2025/07/26/surat-man-duped-of-₹31-lakh-in-fake-tata-zudio-franchise-scam-2-held)
- Patna, 2025: ₹11.79 lakh lost to a fake **Burger King** franchise found via Google. [Free Press Journal](https://www.freepressjournal.in/business/fake-car-dealership-websites-busted-patna-man-arrested)
- Adilabad: ₹3.78 lakh fake fertiliser franchise; police linked the gang to ~500 crimes nationwide. [Telangana Today](https://telanganatoday.com/adilabad-five-held-for-duping-man-of-rs-3-78-lakh-in-fake-fertiliser-franchise-scam)
- Delhi, 2021: fake Haldiram and other franchise sites, ~126 incidents in 16 states; fake sites surfaced as Google results. [Tribune](https://www.tribuneindia.com/news/delhi/4-held-for-running-fake-websites-of-popular-brands-duping-people-by-promising-dealership-307127/amp)
- Brands now publish warnings (Rameshwaram Cafe on fake LOIs; Realme says it charges no registration fee). [Franchise India](https://www.franchiseindia.com/insights/en/news/fake-rameshwaram-cafe-franchise-deals-surface-brand-issues-public-warning.600078)
- **India has no franchise law and no mandatory pre-contract disclosure** (no FDD, no cooling-off); the Contract Act 1872 governs. [Legal 500 India guide](https://www.legal500.com/guides/wp-content/uploads/sites/1/2025/10/India-Franchise-Licensing.pdf)

**The fraud pattern**: web-only presence, staged "registration / NOC / agreement" fees paid online before any meeting, pressure to pay fast, big returns, no physical office or a virtual one, contact through ads and reels.

**Inference**
- Our buyer has read these stories. **Every fraud marker we avoid is a trust signal we earn.** Write the page as the visible opposite of the scam: we meet you first, you see the office, you read the agreement before paying, no fee to "register", payment only against a signed agreement and a GST invoice.
- Because India requires no disclosure, **voluntary disclosure is a costly signal** in Spence's sense. A plain "what you get / what you pay / what we don't promise" list is rare in this category and will stand out.
- Consider a short "How to check us" box: GSTIN and Udyam numbers with links to the government lookups, office address on Google Maps, the founder's name. Fraudsters can't survive verification; we can. (Owner call needed on what to show; GSTIN and Udyam are already in `src/site.ts`.)

### 2.2 Trust signals that matter here

Signals ranked by how hard they are to fake (costly-signal logic, Spence 1973; Gallini & Lutz 1992 applied signalling to franchisors, though Lafontaine 1993, *JLEO* 9(2), found weak support for some signalling predictions in US data, so treat signalling as a lens, not a law):

| Signal | Cost to fake | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Visit our office / meet the founder | Very high | The strongest. Scams avoid meetings. |
| See a live job | Very high | Owner call needed. |
| Named institutional / government clients, with logos | High | Already permitted per PRODUCT.md. Checkable. |
| 18 years (since 2008) | High | Use "since 2008" (concrete) over "15+ years". |
| GSTIN, Udyam, ISO certificate with number | Medium–high | Show the numbers, link the lookups. |
| Named founder with a real photo | Medium | Real photo only (INFO-BANK §9 forbids generated). |
| Sample agreement / clear fee structure | Medium | Strong because rare. Owner call. |
| Real photos of our crew, kit, tanks | Medium | NN/g: real photos are looked at, stock ignored. |
| Polished design | Low | Necessary floor (Fogg et al. 2003) but not proof. |
| Badges, adjectives, ROI % | Very low | Read as hype in this category. |

**Language**: the KPMG–Google 2017 report found Indian-language internet users would outnumber English users 2.5 to 1 by 2021 and that most preferred local-language content ([KPMG](https://kpmg.com/ky/en/home/insights_new/2017/04/indian-language-internet-users.html)). I found **no primary source** for a precise "trust rises X% in local language" figure; don't quote one. Inference: Hindi and Punjabi pages are expected, and for the family reader they may be the only version read.

**WhatsApp**: about 536 million users in India (DataReportal, Feb 2025, third-party). Meta-commissioned Kantar surveys report 91% of online Indian adults talk to a business on WhatsApp and 70% prefer messaging to email or calls (2022). Meta-funded; cite with that caveat. [Summary](https://hyperleap.ai/blog/whatsapp-statistics-india-2026) Inference: WhatsApp is the primary CTA; a form is secondary.

## 3. Behavioural science that applies

Each item: the finding, then how it applies.

1. **Loss aversion.** Kahneman & Tversky (1979), "Prospect theory", *Econometrica* 47(2), 263–291; Tversky & Kahneman (1991), *QJE* 106(4), 1039–1061: losses weigh about twice as much as equal gains. *Applies:* the ₹3–8 lakh is felt as a potential loss far more than the projected gain is felt as a gain. Lead with **risk reduction** (training, support, meeting first, written terms) not upside. Avoid loss-framed fear ("don't miss out"); it is the fraudster's register.
2. **Uncertainty reduction.** Berger & Calabrese (1975), *Human Communication Research* 1(2): people seek information to reduce uncertainty before committing to a relationship. *Applies:* concrete answers to "what happens after I pay" (week by week) reduce uncertainty more than benefit lists.
3. **Signalling.** Spence (1973), "Job market signaling", *QJE* 87(3), 355–374: a signal persuades when it would be too costly for a low-quality sender. *Applies:* §2.2 table.
4. **Trust = ability + benevolence + integrity.** Mayer, Davis & Schoorman (1995), *Academy of Management Review* 20(3), 709–734. *Applies:* ability = process, clients, ISO, years; benevolence = "we'll tell you if your town isn't right", support after payment; integrity = written terms, disclaimers, no hype, consistent figures. Most franchise pages only show ability.
5. **Two-sided messages.** Eisend (2006), "Two-sided advertising: a meta-analysis", *IJRM* 23(2), 187–198: two-sided messages raise source credibility; the effect on attitude is **curvilinear** (some negative information helps, too much hurts). Crowley & Hoyer (1994), *JCR* 20(4), 561–574, give the framework: best when the negatives are on less important attributes and are followed by a refutation or context. [Record](https://data.zhenzhi.com/details/1/862192765) *Applies:* a short "Is this for you?" and "What's hard about this business" section, each point paired with how we help. Two or three honest negatives, not ten.
6. **Pratfall.** Aronson, Willerman & Floyd (1966), *Psychonomic Science* 4(6), 227–228: a blunder made a **highly competent** person more likable and an average one less. Later work is mixed. *Applies:* admitting limits only works after competence is established. Order: proof first, candour second.
7. **Elaboration Likelihood Model.** Petty & Cacioppo (1986), *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology* 19, 123–205: high involvement means people process arguments centrally; argument quality matters, cues matter less. *Applies:* this is a high-involvement buyer. Detail wins (numbers, steps, documents). Peripheral cues (design, faces) still matter for the skimmer and the first impression.
8. **Concreteness and precision.** Hansen & Wänke (2010), "Truth from language and truth from fit", *PSPB* 36(11), 1576–1588 (cited from secondary sources; full text not checked here): concrete statements are judged more likely true. Xie & Kronrod (2012), *Journal of Advertising* 41(4), 103–117: precise numbers made a firm seem more competent, but **mostly for low-sceptic readers**; sceptics were less moved. [Record](https://econbiz.de/Record/is-the-devil-in-the-details-the-signaling-effect-of-numerical-precision-in-environmental-advertising-claims-xie-guang-xin/10009762979) *Applies:* "since 2008", "a 150 sq ft room or your home", "X days of training at our Kharar office" beat "years of experience", "low infrastructure", "full training". Precision in **facts** builds trust; false precision in **projections** (e.g. "47.3% ROI") would backfire with the sceptic.
9. **Processing fluency.** Reber & Schwarz (1999), *Consciousness and Cognition* 8(3), 338–342: easier-to-read statements are judged more true. *Applies:* plain words, short sentences, readable Devanagari and Gurmukhi type, no jargon walls.
10. **Anchoring.** Tversky & Kahneman (1974), *Science* 185, 1124–1131. *Applies:* the first number the reader sees colours the rest. If it is "40% ROI" the page reads as a sales pitch. If it is "from about ₹3 lakh, here is what that covers" it reads as a plan. Anchor on **what the money buys**, not on returns. Don't anchor high to make ₹3 lakh look cheap; that is a manipulation a sceptic notices.
11. **Choice architecture for models.** Huber, Payne & Puto (1982), *JCR* 9(1), 90–98, decoy effect; Frederick, Lee & Baskin (2014), *JMR* 51(4), 487–507, found the decoy effect weak or absent with realistic, non-numeric stimuli. Choice overload (Iyengar & Lepper 2000, *JPSP* 79(6)) had a near-zero mean effect in meta-analysis (Scheibehenne, Greifeneder & Todd 2010, *JCR* 37(3), 409–425). Defaults matter (Johnson & Goldstein 2003, *Science* 302, 1338–1339). *Applies:* two or three models, each with what it includes, are easier to compare than a range. Don't build decoys. A "most people start here" marker is acceptable only if true. Today INFO-BANK forbids stating what each model includes, so the page can only say "₹3–8 lakh depending on model". That is the biggest content gap.
12. **Regulatory focus.** Higgins (1997), "Beyond pleasure and pain", *American Psychologist* 52(12), 1280–1300: promotion-focused people respond to gains and aspirations, prevention-focused people to safety and duties. *Applies:* our main buyer (savings at stake, family watching) is **prevention-focused**. Lead with safety, duty and security ("a steady, respectable business", "written terms", "we train you"); keep aspiration ("step beyond jobs") as the hook, not the argument.
13. **Commitment / foot-in-the-door.** Freedman & Fraser (1966), *JPSP* 4(2), 195–202; Burger (1999) meta-analysis, *PSPR* 3(4), 303–325: small first requests increase later compliance, with modest effects. *Applies:* the CTA ladder: read, then save the summary or share with family, then WhatsApp a question, then a call with the founder, then a visit. Each step small and reversible. Ethical line: each step must be a real step the buyer would want, not a trap.
14. **Fogg Behavior Model.** Fogg (2009), "A behavior model for persuasive design", *Persuasive '09*: behaviour = motivation × ability × prompt at the same moment. *Applies:* place a low-effort prompt (WhatsApp) right after high-motivation moments (after the support section, after the money section, at the end). Make ability high: one tap, prefilled message in the reader's language.
15. **"Imagine yourself" / mental simulation.** Escalas (2004), *Journal of Advertising* 33(2), 37–48: mental simulation and narrative transportation can increase persuasion. Endowment effect (Kahneman, Knetsch & Thaler 1990, *JPE* 98(6), 1325–1348): people value what they feel they own. *Applies:* "A week in your business" (morning site visit, a society job, AMC follow-up), with the Authorised Service Provider name slot shown as "Clean Tank Services, Authorised Service Provider: [Your name], [Your town]". Risk: simulation of income is off-limits; simulate the work, not the money.
16. **Social proof and its limits.** Cialdini (2021), *Influence, New and Expanded*, Harper Business; Cialdini (2003), *Current Directions in Psychological Science* 12(4), 105–109: social proof works best from similar others; inventing or inflating it backfires. *Applies:* we have no partner network to cite. Transfer proof from clients (§8). Never invent testimonials or counts.
17. **Authority.** Cialdini (2021). *Applies:* ISO, government clients, the founder's 18 years. Authority must be real and checkable; fake "approved by govt" lines are a fraud tell.
18. **Hick's law.** Hick (1952), *Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology* 4(1), 11–26: decision time rises with the number of choices. *Applies:* one primary CTA per screen; a short form.
19. **Money-back / exit terms as a signal.** Moorthy & Srinivasan (1995), *Marketing Science* 14(4), 442–466: money-back guarantees can signal quality when they are costly to a low-quality seller. *Applies:* if the owners offer any refund or exit term (e.g. refund of the fee if we cancel before training), state it plainly. It would be the single strongest de-risking line on the page. Only if real.

## 4. Web credibility and conversion evidence

- **Fogg et al. (2003)**, "How do users evaluate the credibility of Web sites? A study with over 2,500 participants", *DUX 2003*: 2,684 people rated live sites; "design look" was mentioned in **46.1% of comments**, more than any other factor; information design/structure 28.5%, information focus 25.1%. Note: of comments, not of people. [Consumer Reports summary](https://advocacy.consumerreports.org/research/how-do-people-evaluate-a-web-sites-credibility) Inference: a shabby page loses before the argument starts. Design is the entry fee.
- **Stanford Guidelines for Web Credibility (Fogg 2002)**: make it easy to verify accuracy; show a real organisation behind the site (address, photos); highlight expertise; show honest, trustworthy people; make contact easy; look professional; keep it useful and current; avoid errors and ads. (Ten guidelines, Stanford Persuasive Tech Lab.) Each maps onto our page.
- **NN/g, "Trustworthiness in web design: 4 credibility factors"** (Harley 2016, re-testing Nielsen 1999): design quality; **upfront disclosure** of everything that affects the customer, including pricing; comprehensive, current content; connection to the rest of the web. Basic trust factors held across cultures. [NN/g](https://nngroup.com/articles/trustworthy-design/) Inference: hiding the investment behind a form reads as a trick. Show the range on the page.
- **NN/g, "Photos as web content"** (Nielsen 2010, eye-tracking): users study photos that carry information, including **real people**, and ignore generic stock people and decorative images. [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/photos-as-web-content/) Inference: real crew, kit, tanks, office, founder. No stock "happy entrepreneur".
- **Forms, Baymard Institute**: average checkout has 11.3 fields; most need about 8; usability falls as fields rise. [Baymard](https://baymard.com/blog/checkout-flow-average-form-fields) This is e-commerce evidence; I found no rigorous public study for lead forms. Multi-step form uplift claims (CXL, Venture Harbour) are vendor case studies, not controlled research. Inference: name, phone (WhatsApp), town, rough budget band, and an optional message. Nothing more on the first step. DPDP consent text is mandatory (PRODUCT.md).
- **Speed to lead, Oldroyd, McElheran & Elkington (2011)**, "The short life of online sales leads", *HBR* 89(3), 28: of 2,241 US firms audited, 23% never responded; average response among responders was 42 hours; firms that responded within an hour were about **7 times** as likely to qualify the lead as those that waited longer. [BYU record](https://scholarsarchive.byu.edu/facpub/9711) Inference: promise and keep a response time ("we reply on WhatsApp the same working day"). A fast human reply is itself a trust signal against fraud fears.
- **Video, founder photos, trust badges**: I found popular claims ("video lifts conversion 80%", "trust badges lift 42%") but **no primary controlled source**. Unsourced; don't quote. Logic still supports a short founder video in Hindi/Punjabi as a costly, personal signal.
- **FAQs / objection handling**: no rigorous study found on FAQ sections and conversion. The case rests on uncertainty reduction (§3.2) and NN/g's upfront-disclosure finding.

### The status question (cleaning work and caste)

**Found**
- **Ashforth & Kreiner (1999)**, "'How can you do it?' Dirty work and the challenge of constructing a positive identity", *AMR* 24(3), 413–434: workers in stigmatised jobs keep positive identities through **reframing** (infusing the work with value), **recalibrating** (shifting which parts count), and **refocusing** (attention to non-stigmatised features). [PSU record](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/how-can-you-do-it-dirty-work-and-the-challenge-of-constructing-a-/)
- India's **NAMASTE** scheme (2023) and Delhi's 2019 "sani-entrepreneur" programme reframe sanitation workers as **owners of mechanised enterprises**. Critics (activists, a policy analysis) argue ownership and machines do not break the link between caste and the work. [Scroll](https://scroll.in/article/992483/delhi-is-trying-to-end-manual-scavenging-by-using-sewer-cleaning-machines-are-its-efforts-working), [Undark](https://undark.org/article/sewer-robots-combat-sewer-scavenging-india/)

**Inference**
- The stigma is real and the buyer's family may raise it in words or silently. Our honest reframes, each true:
  - **Reframe (value):** drinking water hygiene, family health, the founder's "water is life" message.
  - **Recalibrate (role):** the partner runs a **business**: sales, scheduling, client relationships with schools and offices, managing a trained crew. The page should say plainly what the owner does and what the crew does. Owner call: is the partner expected to clean tanks personally?
  - **Refocus (technology and standards):** mechanised equipment, a documented multi-step process, ISO, reports for institutions, uniforms and kit.
- **Do not** use caste language, "dignity" slogans, or anything that implies the work is beneath someone. Write about the business and its standards; let the photos show professionals in uniform with machines.
- Ethical note: the crew who do the physical work deserve respect on the page too. Don't frame "you won't have to do the dirty part" in a way that demeans them.

## 6. Dark patterns, law and ethics

**Found**
- **Consumer Protection Act 2019**, s.2(28) defines misleading advertisement; **CCPA Guidelines for Prevention of Misleading Advertisements and Endorsements, 9 June 2022**, require truthful, honest representation and bar exaggerating accuracy or usefulness. [Acuity Law summary](https://acuitylaw.co.in/md/ccpa-guidelines-an-effort-to-regulate-misleading-advertisements/)
- **CCPA Guidelines for Prevention and Regulation of Dark Patterns, 30 Nov 2023**: name specified dark patterns including **false urgency** (invented scarcity or time pressure), basket sneaking, confirm shaming, forced action, disguised ads, nagging and others; apply to advertisers and sellers, not just platforms. [Vikaspedia](https://en.vikaspedia.in/viewcontent/social-welfare/social-awareness/consumer-education/the-guidelines-for-prevention-and-regulation-of-dark-patterns-2023)
- **ASCI Code, Chapter I** "Truthful and honest representation": claims must be substantiated; misleading by ambiguity, omission or exaggeration is barred. [ASCI](https://www.ascionline.in/?p=10807)
- International benchmark, not binding here: the US **FTC Franchise Rule**, Item 19 (16 CFR 436.5(s)), allows financial performance representations only with a **reasonable basis and written substantiation**, and requires the basis to be disclosed. A good ethical standard for our ROI and payback lines.

**Inference: why fake urgency and income guarantees backfire with this buyer**
- They match the scam pattern in §2.1. A prevention-focused buyer reads urgency as a threat.
- Guarantees invite legal exposure (CPA 2019) and later conflict when reality differs (Weaven et al. 2010).
- Two-sided honesty (Eisend 2006) and upfront disclosure (NN/g) outperform hype for credibility.

## 7. How to present the money

### 7.1 Principles
1. **Show it on the page**, not behind a form (NN/g upfront disclosure).
2. **Lead with what the money buys**, then the range, then the running costs, then the indicative projections, then the disclaimer, all in one visual block. The disclaimer sits *inside* the block, same type size as body text, not in grey footnote type.
3. **Monthly, rupee language** for this buyer (2010 survey: older investors compare to bank interest or monthly income). But we can't show income figures beyond the approved table. So explain the **drivers** instead: "Your earnings depend on how many tanks you clean each month, your prices, and your costs: staff, travel, materials and the 3.5% monthly royalty."
4. **Range with conditions**: "₹3–8 lakh, depending on the model you choose" plus "what changes the cost" (model, territory, equipment).
5. **No percentages as headlines.** ROI and payback appear in the table, never in a hero or a big-number tile.
6. **Show the risks next to the numbers.** "What can make results lower: a slow start, local competition, fewer bookings in some months."

### 7.2 Suggested shape

> **What it takes to start**
> Investment: ₹3 lakh to ₹8 lakh, depending on the model. Most partners can start from about ₹3 lakh. [What each model includes: owner to supply]
> Space: a room of about 150 sq ft, or your home.
> Ongoing: a royalty of 3.5% a month, as applicable to the model. Your own running costs: staff, travel, materials.
> Agreement: 3 years.
> Indicative return: 40% or above. Indicative payback: 4 to 6 months. Both depend on actual business performance.
> [Financial disclaimer, full text, same size]
> Want to see how these numbers could work in your town? We'll go through them with you, in person or on a call.

### 7.3 A credibility problem in the current figures (raise with owners)

- **Indicative ROI 40%+** and **payback 4–6 months** don't fit together if ROI is annual. Paying back the whole investment in 4–6 months implies an annual return of roughly 200–300%. INFO-BANK says what ROI is measured on and over what period is "not stated anywhere".
- A careful reader (or the CA uncle) will notice. In the trust frame, an internal inconsistency is worse than a modest number: it looks like hype. (Integrity, Mayer et al. 1995.)
- Also, a 4–6 month payback sits closer to the competitor claims we want to contrast with (1-month break-even) than to a conservative norm. Unsourced comparison; I have no Indian benchmark study for service franchise payback.
- **Ask the owners**: what is ROI measured on (investment? per year? per month?), what is payback based on (an actual partner's numbers or a model?), and can we show the assumptions (jobs per month, average price, costs)? If they can't be substantiated in writing, consider dropping them from the page and offering "we'll work through the numbers for your town" instead. The FTC Item 19 standard (reasonable basis, written substantiation) is a good test even though it isn't Indian law.

## 8. Substituting for missing partner social proof

We can't cite a network (PRODUCT.md and the brief forbid partner counts and "be among the first"). Transfer proof from what exists:

1. **Operating history:** "Cleaning tanks since 2008." Concrete beats "15+ years".
2. **Clients as proof the system works:** named institutional and government clients with logos; one line: "The same process, the same equipment, the same training our own teams use for [client type]."
3. **Client testimonials** (already in Payload for other pages) framed as proof of the *service* the partner will sell. Do not present them as franchise testimonials.
4. **Documents:** ISO certificate (number and scope), GSTIN, Udyam, a sample training outline, a sample job report given to institutions.
5. **Process as proof:** link to `/process`; show the step track. A documented process is what a partner is buying.
6. **The founder:** name, photo, short story, a direct line: "I meet every partner before we sign." (Only if true.)
7. **Office and live job visit:** "Visit our office in Kharar. See a job in progress." Highest-cost signal available. (Owner call.)
8. **Response promise:** a stated reply time that is kept.
9. **Honesty as proof:** the "who this is not for" section signals we turn people away.

## 12. Tone and wording per language

**All languages:** structured, calm, specific. Short sentences. Rupees, not percentages, where possible. Same facts, same figures, same disclaimer in all three.

**English**
- Prefer: business, partner, system, training, support, process, standards, written agreement, your town, indicative, depends on.
- Avoid: guaranteed, assured, passive income, easy money, high profit, fast ROI, limited slots, hurry, be among the first, exclusive offer, life-changing, financial freedom, 100%, best, #1, risk-free, zero risk, "All in package" (undefined, per INFO-BANK).

**Hindi**
- Register: plain, respectful (आप), everyday Hindi, not Sanskritised. Use common English loanwords that people actually say: ट्रेनिंग, सपोर्ट, फ्रेंचाइज़ी, एग्रीमेंट.
- Prefer: अपना व्यवसाय, लिखित एग्रीमेंट, ट्रेनिंग, सपोर्ट, अनुमानित (indicative), "आपके कारोबार पर निर्भर करता है".
- Avoid: गारंटीड कमाई, पक्की कमाई, घर बैठे कमाएं, लाखों कमाएं, सीमित सीटें, जल्दी करें, मौका हाथ से न जाने दें, शत-प्रतिशत. "घर बैठे" in particular is the language of work-from-home scams.

**Punjabi (Gurmukhi)**
- Register: warm but respectful (ਤੁਸੀਂ), standard Gurmukhi, with familiar loanwords.
- Prefer: ਆਪਣਾ ਕਾਰੋਬਾਰ, ਲਿਖਤੀ ਇਕਰਾਰਨਾਮਾ / ਐਗਰੀਮੈਂਟ, ਟ੍ਰੇਨਿੰਗ, ਸਹਾਇਤਾ, ਅੰਦਾਜ਼ਨ.
- Avoid: ਪੱਕੀ ਕਮਾਈ, ਗਾਰੰਟੀ, ਘਰ ਬੈਠੇ ਕਮਾਓ, ਜਲਦੀ ਕਰੋ, ਸੀਮਤ ਮੌਕਾ.

The `content-updater` should check translations with a native reader for scam-sounding phrasing; that check is about connotation, which machine translation misses.

## 14. Questions for the owners

Psychology depends on these answers. Each should become its own Track Suru issue.

1. **Models:** what does each model (₹3 lakh vs ₹8 lakh) include? Equipment, training days, kit, marketing, territory? (Biggest gap; INFO-BANK currently forbids stating it.)
2. **ROI and payback:** what are they measured on and over what period? Based on an actual partner or a projection? Can we show assumptions? (See §7.3: as written they look inconsistent.)
3. **Royalty:** 3.5% monthly of what (revenue, a fixed base)? Is there a minimum?
4. **Other costs:** any joining fee, deposit, consumables bought from us, software, uniforms, GST on fees? Can we say "no other charges" truthfully?
5. **Payment and paperwork:** is payment only after a signed agreement, against a GST invoice? Any token amount before? Can we publish "no registration fee"?
6. **Refund and exit:** any refund before training? What happens at the end of 3 years, or if the partner wants out early? Equipment buy-back?
7. **Meeting:** will the CEO meet every serious prospect, in person or on video? Can families come?
8. **Office visit and live job:** can prospects visit Kharar and watch a live job?
9. **Territory:** is the territory defined? Exclusive? (INFO-BANK forbids stating exclusivity; the buyer will ask.)
10. **Leads:** what does "lead generation support may be provided" mean in practice? Do we pass on enquiries from the website for their area?
11. **Role:** is the partner expected to clean tanks personally, or to run a crew? Minimum crew size?
12. **Training:** how many days, where, by whom, what's covered, any certificate?
13. **Existing partners:** are there any (even one)? Would they speak to prospects? (Would change the social-proof plan entirely.)
14. **Response time:** what reply time can we promise on WhatsApp?
15. **Who should apply:** who would the owners turn away? (Feeds "not for you".)
16. **Verification box:** OK to show GSTIN, Udyam and ISO numbers with lookup links on this page?
17. **Sample agreement:** can a prospect see the agreement (or a summary) before the meeting?
