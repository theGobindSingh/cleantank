## 0. Short version

1. **The five ideas in the brief are not equally strong.** Reciprocity has good causal field evidence, but only for *unconditional* gifts. Foot-in-the-door is small and recent direct replications failed. The IKEA effect is causal but comes from lab tasks with Lego and origami. Psychological ownership and franchisee autonomy evidence is correlational. The belonging interventions were tested on US college students and give gains of about two percentage points, in supportive settings only. None of the five was tested in Many Labs 1 to 5, so "replication status" for most of them means single replications or meta-analyses, which run about three times larger than preregistered replications (Kvarven et al. 2020).
2. **The evidence argues against gating.** The reciprocity research that works in the field (Falk 2007; Church 1993) gives first and asks later. Gifts promised in exchange for something (Church's "promised incentives") did not significantly raise response. A guide behind a phone-number form is a trade, not a gift. So: the guide, the preview and the worksheet are free and ungated. Contact is asked for only when the prospect wants something only a person can give: a review of their plan, a call or a visit.
3. **Reciprocity buys a reply, not a ₹5.5 lakh decision.** Effects are small and fade within days (Burger et al. 1997). Anyone who signs out of obligation for a free PDF is a lead we should not want.
4. **The strongest support for the design comes from two sources the brief did not name.** Realistic job previews (Earnest, Allen & Landis 2011: 52 studies, about 17,000 people) work mainly by making the organisation look honest, and help people self-select out. Process simulation (Taylor et al. 1998) beats imagining success, and positive fantasies about outcomes predict *less* effort (Oettingen & Mayer 2002). The preview should show the work and what is hard about it, never the money.
5. **The worksheet's real job is information for both sides, not persuasion.** Co-creation can backfire: when a co-created result fails, people are more dissatisfied than if they had not taken part (Heidenreich et al. 2015). A prospect who spends an hour on a plan and is then declined will feel worse. Keep it short (about 10 minutes), make the output useful on its own, and publish the criteria first so people rule themselves out before they invest effort.
6. **Belonging is the riskiest idea here.** "Join the Clean Tank family" is the register of MLM and of the franchise frauds in CT-409 §2.1. PRODUCT.md also forbids implying any partner network. The real belonging group for this buyer is their own family. Serve that (CT-421) instead of manufacturing a community.
7. **Autonomy support is the most useful idea and the cheapest:** say plainly that the prospect can stop at any point, show what is fixed (the five steps, the brand) and what is theirs to decide. The "but you are free" meta-analysis is positive overall but shows no effect in the seven low-risk-of-bias studies (Meta-Psychology re-analysis), so use it because it is honest, not because it lifts conversion.

Indian field work on reciprocity exists (a large HelpAge-related charity experiment listed by Yale's Cowles Foundation; Chung & Narayandas on gift-style pay for Indian sales staff) but I could not read either, so I don't rely on them. No study anywhere tests any of these ideas on franchise buyers.

### Use

## 3. Where the client's thesis holds and where it doesn't

The thesis in CT-442: early voluntary contact capture, educational reciprocity, real co-creation, two-way screening.

- **Holds:** value before commitment, two-way screening, human review, no payment to apply. The RJP and two-sided message evidence supports these better than the reciprocity evidence does.
- **Weak:** "reciprocity leads to contact capture". The causal evidence says gifts work when unconditional. Capture tied to the gift is the condition that didn't work in Church 1993. Expect reciprocity to raise goodwill and replies, not the opt-in rate.
- **Weak:** "foot-in-the-door builds commitment". Small effect, failed recent replications, mostly prosocial requests. Small steps are still right, for ease and lower risk, not for commitment.
- **Partly holds:** "co-creation creates ownership". Causal in the lab, untested in franchising, and with a known backfire. Its value here is the information it gives both sides.
- **Doesn't hold as written:** "belonging". No evidence that belonging messages move a purchase like this, and the obvious copy breaks PRODUCT.md and looks like MLM.
- **Contradiction to flag for CT-448:** the opt-in rate is the wrong measure of any of this. CT-447's qualified-applications KPI is the right one.

## 6. Readiness worksheet: spec

**Name for the page:** working title "Your local business plan" (avoids "readiness", which sounds like a test). Final label needs the owner, since PRODUCT.md has an approved CTA list.

**How it works, in two parts:**

1. **Fill in, privately.** The worksheet runs in the browser. Nothing is sent or stored while the prospect fills it in. At the end they get their own one-page summary (§7.1) to save, print or forward. No contact details asked.
2. **Send for review, only if they choose.** A separate step, "Send my plan to Clean Tank for review", asks for name and mobile and the consent below. Two routes:
   - **WhatsApp (fits today's PRODUCT.md rule, "enquiry is by call or WhatsApp only"):** opens WhatsApp to the main number with the answers pre-filled as text. The prospect presses send. Caution: the`wa.me` link then holds their answers, so the button must sit inside`ph-no-capture`, and PostHog autocapture must not record its`href` (CT-446 §7.4).
   - **Form (needs the owner to change "There is no franchise form", CT-446 §10 item 1):** stores the worksheet with the lead, under the retention rules in CT-446 §7.6.

It is offered at S2 (after the intro call, as in CT-446) and also openly on the page and in the guide, for people who want to think first.

Target: about 10 minutes, twelve questions, one per screen on a phone, back button, progress shown as "4 of 12" in text (not colour only). Every choice list includes "Not sure yet". Latin letters accepted in every language (CT-443).

### 6.2 How answers feed human review

1. The franchise lead (owner to name, CT-446 Q1) reads the worksheet before the call or within the SLA's 3 working days (CT-446 §3).
2. They note, per rubric row, what the answer shows and what to ask. The worksheet alone never decides: gates are checked at S1, S3 and S4, and scores are given only at S4 by two people.
3. Reviewer rules:
   - Read for specificity, not ambition. "Three societies on my road" beats "all of Ludhiana".
   - Expect people to rate their own plan highly (C1). Look at what they wrote, not how confident it sounds.
   - "Not sure yet" is a fine answer. It becomes a call topic.
   - Never infer anything from the "we do not look at" list in CT-446 §1.3 (caste, religion, gender, marital status, age beyond adulthood).
   - Write one strong point and one or two open points for the feedback. Keep the notes with the lead, never in analytics.
4. The answers go nowhere else: not to other partners, not to service marketing, not into PostHog or Sentry (CT-446 §7.4).

## 7. What the prospect gets back

### 7.1 Straight away, without sending anything

A one-page "Your plan" summary, built in the browser from their own answers:

- Their answers, tidied into a page they can print, save as PDF or forward.
- The model(s) whose total sits inside their budget band, with the approved summary and the disclaimer beside it. This is a plain lookup against the three totals, not advice and not a score.
- "Things still open for you": every "Not sure yet" turned into a question to think about or ask us.
- "Check locally": the homework steps from guide section 12, filled in with the customer types they picked.
- "Questions to ask us": three or four from guide section 11, chosen by their answers (e.g. other costs, if they picked Below ₹5.5 lakh or Not sure).
- A family version: the same page without the funding answers, for forwarding (CT-421). Generated on the device. No answers are put in a URL.
- The two buttons, and the line: "If you send this to us, a person reads it and replies within [SLA]. You can stop at any time."

What it never shows: a score, a percentage, "you qualify", any earnings, demand or market-size figure, or a territory promise.

### 7.2 After sending, from a person, within the SLA

Written feedback in their language, on the channel they picked (draft structure for CT-448 to word):

1. Thanks, by name, and the one strongest thing in their plan.
2. Their town: open, needs a territory check, or not open now. Never "reserved for you".
3. Which model fits what they told us, and why, in one or two lines. Or why none fits yet.
4. One or two things missing or worth checking, with the reason.
5. The next step we offer: a call at a time they pick, a visit to Kharar (if CT-418 allows), or a hold with the named gap and a date (CT-446 §4).
6. The anti-fraud line (CT-419): we never ask for payment to apply, and never to a personal account or UPI ID.
7. The data line: what we keep, for how long, and how to ask us to delete it (CT-446 §7.6).

This feedback is the reciprocity that matters most here: personal, specific, useful even if they stop, and given before we ask for anything bigger.

### 7.3 What to measure (for CT-447)

Counted from the lead store, not analytics: worksheets sent, share of sends after the intro call against cold sends, time to feedback against the SLA, share of senders reaching S3, and decline and withdrawal reasons. Worksheet opens and completions can be counted in PostHog as bare events with no answer values.

## 9. Limits

- Several papers were read only as abstracts or secondary summaries: Burger 1999, Carpenter 2013, Earnest et al. 2011, Church 1993, Heidenreich et al. 2015, Zlatev & Rogers 2020. Effect sizes for those come from the abstract or a named summary. Standard citations (Taylor et al. 1998, Pham & Taylor 1999, Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006, Plott & Zeiler 2005, Goei et al. 2003, Burger et al. 1997, Gneezy & List 2006, Genschow et al. 2021, Staw 1976) are from their bibliographic records.
- No Many Labs project tested reciprocity, foot-in-the-door, the IKEA effect, psychological ownership or belonging. Many Labs 1 replicated sunk cost and anchoring; Many Labs 2 replicated 15 of 28 effects (54%) with 75% of effects smaller than the originals (Klein et al. 2018, *AMPPS* 1(4)).
- Nothing here is tested on Indian franchise buyers. The worksheet itself is the first chance to learn how they answer.
